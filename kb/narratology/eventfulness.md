# Eventfulness and Tellability

**Concept:** eventfulness · **Layer:** narratology
**Used by:** `adversarial-reader` agent, `narrative-architect` agent

## Definition
Not every happening is an *event* worth telling. **Tellability** (Labov's "so what?" test) and
**eventfulness** (Hühn) ask what makes a change narratable: it must be a *significant* change of
state that *deviates from the expected script* and carries a point. A canonical, predicted
happening ("she woke, dressed, caught the train") has near-zero eventfulness; a change that
violates the default course of things — and matters to someone — is high. This is the formal
core of the "non-boring" requirement: boredom is low eventfulness.

## Use when
Testing whether a scene/beat earns its place (the anti-boredom check), and when choosing which
of several possible next developments to pursue.

## Diagnostic questions
- After this beat, could a reader answer "so what?" without shrugging?
- Is this a *deviation* from the expected script, or the script itself?
- Does the change matter to a character who wanted otherwise? (Mattering = stakes.)
- Is the most *canonical* (expected) continuation the one I chose? Then eventfulness is low —
  find a motivated deviation ([[suspense-surprise-curiosity]], [[defaultness]]).

## Failure modes
- "Errand" scenes: motion without a state-change that matters.
- Canonical happenings narrated at scene length (should be summary, or cut).
- Deviation for its own sake (unmotivated shock) — high surprise, zero necessity.

## Conflicts with
Quiet/slice-of-life aesthetics — some contracts prize small eventfulness rendered precisely.
Calibrate the threshold to the reader contract, don't maximize shock.
