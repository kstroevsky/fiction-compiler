"How's Mam?"

"Same," Lena said. "No worse."

"She asking after me?"

He never worked up to it, this one. Straight there — the one present he wanted, reached for first.

"You know Mam," Lena said.

"She never misses. Even now, with her head going, she'll —" He tapped the table, pleased, sure of it. "She'll have me name off before your coat's on the peg. Am I right?"

Once he'd have been right. Once Lena would come in at the door of the home and Mam's face would go past her to the hall behind her to see was Danny there, and when he wasn't she'd ask, and how's your brother, when's he coming, is he eating — and Lena would stand there with the shopping and the forms and the washing done, and give her the news of him, the only one of the two of them who came up the country, and Mam would take it and want more.

"You're right," Lena said. "She had your name off before I'd me coat off."

It cost nothing to say and it wasn't true, and she'd said harder true things to easier faces. Two weeks Mam hadn't found his name. This morning she'd asked when Lena was coming with Lena in front of her. But Danny sat there lit up with it, with being the one asked after, the one whose name came first even now, and Lena let him have it, because the other thing was to reach across a table she wasn't allowed to reach across and take it off him, and she'd driven three hours, and she was tired, and he was her brother, and none of those were the same reason.

"Course I am," Danny said. "She's made of old rope, Mam."

"Made of old rope."

"You'll bring us a photo of her? Next time?"

The photo was in Lena's back pocket, printed Sunday — Mam at the glass, the light through her hair, her eyes gone somewhere past the room. "Next time," Lena said, and left it where it was.
