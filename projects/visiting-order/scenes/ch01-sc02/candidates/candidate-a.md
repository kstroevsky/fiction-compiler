"How's Mam keeping?"

There it was. Lena turned her cup a quarter-turn on the table.

"Same," she said. "No worse."

"She getting out to the garden at all?"

"When it's fine. Donna takes her round the beds."

"Donna." He tried the name like it was a thing he could hold. "She likes the roses, Mam. Always did." He drank, and set the cup down, and came to it. He came to it every month, in the end. "She say owt? About me?"

And there was the morning: Mam in the chair by the window, asking when Lena was coming with Lena stood in front of her, and not his name, not for a fortnight, not since she said it to Donna and meant Donna. It would take a sentence. Less than the one about the roses. She doesn't ask any more, Danny. It sat in Lena's mouth with its coat on.

He had the look he'd had from his cot, from the back of a classroom, from the dock — that she would sort it, whatever it was, because she always had, because that was the use of her.

"She was asking," Lena said. "Wanting to know were you eating. I told her you'd got fat as a bishop."

He laughed, the real one, the one that took ten years off him and put them somewhere on her. "She never said that."

"Her words."

"Get away." He pressed the heel of his hand to one eye, still grinning, and for a minute he was easy in the bolted chair, a man whose mother was asking after him, wanting to know was he eating.

Lena had made that. She had made it at a machine-tea table out of nothing at all, and handed it across the table, where you weren't allowed to pass so much as a photograph, and it had done him more good than anything in the locker. It was the best thing she'd brought him in a year and she'd had it off the top of her head.

The photo was in her back pocket. She'd taken it Sunday and had it printed at the machine in Boots — Mam by the window with the light coming through the thin of her hair, looking at something past the camera, past the garden, past the lot of them. You could bring one in to show. She had it two inches from her hand the whole time.

"You'll fetch us a photo," Danny said. "Next time. One of her. I've none in the cell now, they lost me box in the move."

"Next time," Lena said.

Her hand stayed where it was, flat on the table by the cup. Down the hall a buzzer went for somebody else's visit ending, and Danny didn't hear it yet, and they had a while left, the two of them, and the tea going cold, and Mam at the window three hours north not asking after either of them.
