The drive was three hours if the roadworks let you, and they hadn't. Lena put the car in the visitors' lot with twenty minutes to spare and sat a moment with the engine off before she went in.

Inside, she gave Danny's name and her own and the number off the visiting order, and the woman behind the glass ran a finger down a list and slid across a locker key. Everything went in the locker but the coins for the machine and the printed photo. No phone, no bag, no keys. She had done it enough times now to empty her pockets before she was asked.

The search was the same as always. She stood on the marks on the floor with her arms out while the officer patted her down and the dog came along the line and lost interest in her. They stamped the back of her hand with the ink you couldn't see and held it under the lamp to show her it had taken, a smudge that glowed and then was nothing again in the ordinary light. Then the wait on the bolted chairs, with the other women and a few kids and one old man, until they called the visit and let them through the double doors a set at a time.

Danny was at a table near the back. He stood when he saw her, which he always did, and he was smaller than the month before, or she was looking harder. The tracksuit did nobody any favours. They were allowed the one hold at the start. His arms went round her and the shoulder blades stood up through the cotton, and then the officer at the desk stirred at his station and they sat.

"You look well," he said. He said it every time. "Roads bad?"

"Roadworks the whole way past the turn."

"They've been at that stretch since I don't know when."

"Since before," she said, and he laughed like she'd said something clever, and some of it went out of both of them, the first few minutes always the worst.

She had seen Mam that morning. She went most Sundays before the drive, and this Sunday Mam had been in the chair they set her in by the window, and she'd looked at Lena and asked when Lena was coming to see her, and Lena had said she was there, she'd come, she was here now, and Mam had turned back to the glass. She had not asked after Danny. She had not said his name in a fortnight, not since the morning she said it to a care assistant called Donna and meant Donna.

Danny had been the one she asked after. All the years of it. Danny who was in here and never wrote, Danny who'd emptied her purse and worse and been forgiven it before the door shut. Lena came every month. Lena she forgot to ask about to Lena's own face.

"Get us a tea?" Danny said. "I've no change, they docked the account for the—" He waved it off, the canteen, some levy, it didn't matter.

Lena went to the hatch and fed the machine and carried back two teas in the cups that were too hot to hold round the middle, and set them down, and sat. Danny wrapped both hands around his the way he had as a boy at the kitchen table, and looked at her, glad, and she knew what he was going to ask before the tea was cool enough to drink.
