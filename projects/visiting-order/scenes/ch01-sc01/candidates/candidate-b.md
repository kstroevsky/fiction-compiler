Lena was through the search and stamped and sitting on the bolted chairs by ten past, the printed photo warm in her back pocket from the drive. She had decided in the car that she would tell him. She had decided it somewhere past the roadworks and held it the rest of the way like a cup she didn't want to spill.

They called the visit. Through the double doors, one set then the next, into the noise of the hall — the chairs, the tea machine, a child somewhere winding up.

Danny stood when he saw her. Smaller than last month. He always stood; it was a manners thing, from Mam. The one hug they were allowed, his shoulder blades under the cotton, and the officer's eye bringing them down into their seats.

"You look well," he said. "Roads alright?"

"Roadworks past the turn."

"Still? They've been at that since forever."

"Since before," she said, and he laughed, and for a minute it was easier, the first minutes always the worst.

She had gone to Mam that morning. Mam had asked when Lena was coming, with Lena stood in front of her. She had not asked after Danny. Two weeks now she had not said his name, and the last time she said it she had meant the girl who did her tablets.

For a year Lena had come here and carried the other version up the motorway. Mam sends her love. Mam was asking after you. She had set it down on this table once a month like something she'd brought him, and it did him good, and she'd driven home. It had been true enough when she started. It was the thing she had instead of the thing Mam had always handed him for nothing.

"No change for the tea," Danny said. "They docked us. Long story." He smiled it away.

She got the teas. He took his in both hands. He had the look he got when he was working round to something, and the sentence she had carried three hours sat up in her chest, and she still did not know if she would let it out.

He was working round to Mam. It was always Mam.
