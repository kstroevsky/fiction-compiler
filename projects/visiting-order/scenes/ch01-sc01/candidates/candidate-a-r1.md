The drive was three hours if the roadworks let you, and they hadn't. Lena put the car in the visitors' lot with twenty minutes to spare and sat a moment with the engine off before she went in.

Inside, she gave Danny's name and her own and the number off the visiting order, and the woman behind the glass ran a finger down a list and slid across a locker key. Everything went in the locker but the coins for the machine and the printed photo. No phone, no bag, no keys. She had done it enough times now to empty her pockets before she was asked.

The search was the same as always. She stood on the marks on the floor with her arms out while the officer patted her down and the dog came along the line and lost interest in her. They stamped the back of her hand with the ink you couldn't see and held it under the lamp to show her it had taken, there and then gone. Then the wait on the bolted chairs, with the other women and a few kids and one old man, until they called the visit and let them through the double doors a set at a time.

Danny was at a table near the back. He stood when she got to it, which he always did, and he was smaller than the month before, or she was looking harder. The tracksuit did nobody any favours. They were allowed the one hold at the start. His arms went round her and the shoulder blades stood up through the cotton, and then the officer at the desk stirred at his station and they sat.

"You look well," he said. He said it every time. "Roads bad?"

"Roadworks the whole way past the turn."

"They've been at that stretch since I don't know when."

"Since before," she said, and he laughed like she'd said something clever.

She had seen Mam that morning — she went most Sundays before the drive — and this Sunday Mam was in the chair they set her in by the window, and she looked at Lena and asked when Lena was coming to see her, and Lena said she was there, she'd come, she was here now, and Mam turned back to the glass. Mam had not asked after Danny. Had not said his name in a fortnight, not since the morning she said it to a care assistant called Donna and meant Donna.

Danny had been the one she asked after. All the years of it. Danny who was in here and never wrote, Danny who'd emptied her purse more than the once and been forgiven it before the door shut. Lena came every month. Lena she forgot to ask about to Lena's own face.

Every visit Lena brought him the same words. Mam sends her love. Mam was asking after you, wanting to know were you eating. She said them across the table like something fetched from home, and he took them, and she drove back with the car empty. She had them ready now, behind her teeth. Some of the way up, past the roadworks, she'd thought she might not bring them out this time — let him ask, and hold on to them, and see.

"Get us a tea?" Danny said. "I've no change, they docked the account for the—" He waved it off, the canteen, some levy, it didn't matter.

Lena went to the hatch and fed the machine and carried back two teas in the cups that were too hot to hold round the middle, and set them down, and sat. Danny wrapped both hands around his the way he had as a boy at the kitchen table, and looked at her. She knew what he was going to ask before the tea was cool enough to drink.
