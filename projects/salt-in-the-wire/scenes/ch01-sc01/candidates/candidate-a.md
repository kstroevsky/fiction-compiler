The panel had read OFFLINE for as long as the station had been hers, and for years before that. Routine said: open the housing, confirm the break, file the fault, requisition the part. Mara set the screwdriver to the first screw and turned.

Inside, the cable lay in two pieces. The ends did not fray the way weather leaves a wire, splayed and greened where damp has worked it apart across a season. They were flat. A blade had taken the cable in one pass and left two clean faces, and the copper at each face had gone the dull green of old coins.

She put her thumb to it. Cold. The corrosion ran deep instead of surface — a cut with years behind it. Longer than her posting. Longer than the two operators before her, whose initials climbed the spine of the log.

That log lay open on the bench where it always waited, the day's tide already entered in her own hand. Below that line there was room for the fault. Relay housing opened. Cable severed. The sentence was ready, and it was the true one.

Above the bench hung the tide table, its printed columns overwritten in a stranger's small corrections — decades of them, every figure checked against a sea that did not care what the district printed. Someone had kept this station honest by hand for a long time. The same someone had cut the wire that called for help, and never written it down.

Mara left the line unwritten. She fitted the housing back over the two clean ends, set each screw, and the panel went on reading OFFLINE, the way it had been made to.
