Jonas Reell kept a shed above the tide line, and he was in it when Mara came down the harbor road. The door stood open. He had a chart spread on the bench and a pair of dividers in his hand, and he did not look up when her shadow fell across the paper.

"You cut the relay," she said.

He walked the dividers across the chart. "You came a long way to tell a man something he knows."

"Why."

"Close the door. The wind takes my marks."

She closed it. On the bench lay the approach to the point, as familiar to her as her own hand, the reef inked in red a half-cable off the shoal water. Beside it lay a second sheet in his writing, columns of soundings, dates going back before she was born.

"I filed nothing," she said. "The fault. I opened the housing and I closed it again."

"I know that too." He set the dividers down. "You'd not be standing in my shed if you'd filed it."

He turned the second sheet so it faced her, put his thumb on one of his own figures, then on the red mark on the chart, and moved between the two until the gap stood open between them — the reef where the district drew it, and the reef where the water said it stood. Half a cable. The relay called a ship onto the district's line. The district's line ran onto rock.

Mara looked at it for a long moment. Outside, a halyard knocked against a mast, and knocked again.

"You could have told someone," she said.

"I did." He picked the dividers back up. "I told the water, every year, and I wrote down what it answered. Tell the district and they fund a survey in ten years, or they don't, and every season between, the light calls them in." He looked at her then. "You've closed the housing. That's the whole of it. Go home and keep closing it, or go home and file — but don't come here to make me say it was easy."

She did not answer. She had the chart in her eye now, and could not put it down.
