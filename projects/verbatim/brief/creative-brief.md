# Creative Brief — Verbatim

## Core promise
A court interpreter — the only person who can render the hill dialect the witness speaks — realizes
the old man in the box is shading his own words so that a *faithful* translation will convict the
accused, his brother. He will not be seen to condemn his own blood; he is using her tongue as the
blade. She alone decides whether the record hears the damning sense or the innocent one.

## Desired reader experience
Moral vertigo and complicity. By the end the reader should feel the horror not of a villain but of
neutrality itself — that "faithful" and "impartial" cannot both be kept — and should not be able to
say cleanly whether Mira's final rendering was justice or its opposite.

## Theme question
Is a faithful rendering a just one, and can the person in the middle ever be neutral? Stage it in
the choice of a single word; never speak it.

## Genre obligations
Literary, restrained, procedural. The interpreter's discipline is the texture. The tribunal is
invented and unnamed; the "valley" and its dead are the wound.

## Deliberate deviations
- Centers the interpreter, not the lawyer/accused — the powerless middle holds all the power.
- Ends before the verdict, on Mira's act.
- The antagonist is a moral structure (neutrality), not a person.

## Content boundaries
A past atrocity (a flood-and-raid on a village) referred to, never depicted on the page. No graphic
violence. Invented setting; no real ethnicity, nation, or conflict.

## Originality risks (defaults to resist)
- Legal-thriller theatrics; a dramatic verdict; a confession outburst.
- Mira delivering a speech about truth vs. law.
- A tidy moral resolution or a last-line uplift.
- The witness as a mustache-twirling manipulator (he is grieving, not scheming).

## Open questions (for the human gate)
- Which sense does Mira render at the end — and does the text let the reader see it, or only its cost?
- Is Radan (the accused) ever on the page, or only a pressure? (Currently: a presence, barely seen.)
