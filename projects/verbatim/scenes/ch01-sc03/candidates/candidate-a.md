The decisive question came in the last hour, when the light through the high windows had gone the color of the water jug.

Counsel wanted it plain for the record. On the night the raiders came to the sluice, counsel said, did the witness see the accused — and here the accused's name, and the panel leaning the little that panels lean — lead them, or be led? Say what you saw.

Josif took his time. He was good at time. He set his hands on the rail the way he had set nothing else down all day, and looked not at the panel and not at Mira but at the middle distance where the valley was, and he gave her the word.

He gave it with both hands and one meaning. There was no washing it now; he had spent the recess taking the clean half out of her reach, so that when the word came she would have only the lamp left, the man walking ahead in the dark, *he led them*, and all she had to do was what she was sworn to do — which was nothing, which was to carry across exactly what was there.

Mira held the stem of the microphone. In the good ear the word sat and waited, both its hands open.

"The witness uses a word," she said, in the flat register, to the panel, to the record, to the stenographer whose keys had never once been wrong on her account, "that our languages do not have. It means a man who goes ahead in the dark. It does not say whether he goes ahead to lead them, or because they drove him out in front. In his tongue it is one word. I cannot make it two for you. You will have to."

The room took a moment to understand that the interpreter had spoken, and then that she had stopped.

She did not look at Josif. She could feel the valley going out of the length of him, the lamp guttering, the long walk down for nothing, and the other thing arriving in its place — the morning they would give his brother now, the door opening on it. She had kept her oath. She had put the word down true, with both its hands, and let it be light enough to lift a guilty man into the day. No one in the room could say she had chosen. She would know it for the rest of her life as the only thing she had ever truly chosen.
