# Verbatim

## Chapter 1
On the third day Josif spoke of the water, and Mira gave the court his water.

She had his voice in one ear and the chamber in the other, and the half-second between was the only country she governed. He was an old man from the top of the valley, and he spoke the hill way, slow, the sentences turned end to first, so that a listener waited for the verb the way you wait for a stone to reach the bottom of a well. Counsel had asked how the raiders found the upper path. Josif said the path had always been there. He said the water knew it before any man did. Then he came to Radan, and he used the word *vedel*.

*Vedel.* In the court languages it wanted two hands to carry. *He was led to it* — a man swept along, a leaf on the flood. *He led them to it* — a man who walks ahead with a lamp. The dialect held both in one breath and let the breath decide; and Mira, who had been keeping men out of trouble with the cleaner half of a word since she was nineteen, gave the court the leaf. *He was led to it,* she said, in the flat court register, and the keys took it down as though it were the only thing the word had ever meant.

She reached for the water at her elbow and did not drink it.

Because Josif had stopped. Not long — a witness is always stopping, groping for the next stone — but he had turned his head a quarter, off counsel, off the panel, and found her at the low desk where no one looks, and he held there. He did not frown, and he did not plead. He waited, the way a man waits at a door he knows will open, until she understood that he had chosen *vedel* the way she chose its cleaner half — on purpose, for its two hands — and that he had watched her wash it, and that he had come all the way down the valley to be washed.

Counsel said, *And then?* Mira put her thumb to the stem of the microphone. In her good ear the old man was already moving on, laying the next stone, and the next, building toward the sluice with the patience of someone who means to be standing there when the wall comes down. She gave the court his words. She gave them true. And one of them she had given true in the wrong direction, and only two people in the room knew it, and both of them had chosen to.

· · ·

They could not speak. That was the first rule they had taught her and the one the room believed in most: a witness and his voice kept apart in the recess, the way you keep a flame from what it would burn. So when they brought Josif down the back corridor between two officers, and the corridor was narrow, and Mira had flattened herself against the cold wall to let them pass, neither of them said a word the record could have wanted.

He was smaller out of the box. Up close the valley was all over him — the hands, the way the collar sat. He did not look at her. He looked at the water cooler humming against the wall, the paper cones beside it, and he did not take one, and to the officer who offered he said, in the hill way, that a man does not drink where he is going. The officer heard an old man refuse a cup. Mira had the rest of it, the half that lived in the breath: where he was going was nowhere he expected to be given water again.

Then, still to the cooler, still to no one, he said the thing about the lamp.

In the valley they kept a saying for the one who goes ahead in the dark so the others can follow, and it was a kind saying, a saying for shepherds and midwives and the first boat out after a storm. Josif turned it over the way he had turned *vedel*, and gave it its other face — the one who goes ahead with the lamp so the dark has something to follow him to. He said it of no one. He said it to the water cooler, in a dialect the corridor did not keep, and then the officers had him past her and round the corner, and the saying stayed behind in the cold like a coat someone had dropped.

Radan had carried the lamp. Not been led — led. The one witness who could have said so was dead, and the law would weigh what it could hold in its hands and find it light, and let him out into a morning; and Josif knew all of this the way she knew it now, and had known it longer, and had come down out of the valley to set it in the one pair of hands the law could not watch.

Hers.

She stood in the corridor after they had gone and did not go back in. Down the hall a door opened on the sound of the chamber, the flat voices, the shuffle of the panel returning, and closed again. She would have to go in. She had a word waiting for her in there, and now it had only one hand.

· · ·

The decisive question came in the last hour, when the light through the high windows had gone the color of the water jug.

Counsel wanted it plain for the record. On the night the raiders came to the sluice, did the witness see the accused — and here the name, and the panel leaning the little that panels lean — lead them, or be led? Say what you saw.

Josif took his time; he was good at time. He set his hands on the rail the way he had set nothing else down all day, looked not at the panel and not at Mira but at the middle distance where the valley was, and gave her the word.

He gave it with both hands and one meaning. All through his testimony he had been walking the court toward the lamp — the path, the sluice, the man who goes ahead in the dark — so that by the time the word came it could carry only the one sense, *he led them*, and a faithful tongue would carry that and nothing after it. He had built her a word with a single hand and set it in her mouth.

Mira held the stem of the microphone. In the good ear the word waited, and it had two hands after all; she was the only one left in the room who still held the other.

"The witness uses a word," she said, in the flat register, to the panel, to the record, to the stenographer whose keys had never once been wrong on her account, "that our languages do not have. It means a man who goes ahead in the dark. It does not say whether he goes to lead them, or because they drove him out in front. In his tongue it is one word. I cannot make it two for you. You will have to."

The room took a moment to understand that the interpreter had spoken, and then that she had stopped. The stenographer's hands hung over the keys, waiting for a rendering that did not come, and set down what she had said instead.

She drank the water at her elbow; she had not touched it all day. Across the chamber the old man turned his face back to the middle distance, to the valley, to the lamp he had carried down all this way and could set now in no hand but his own. The panel leaned together to confer. In the next hour they would weigh a word she had refused to halve, and a man would go out into the morning or he would not, and the record would carry her words where the word should have stood — in her own voice, which the record had never before had reason to name.
