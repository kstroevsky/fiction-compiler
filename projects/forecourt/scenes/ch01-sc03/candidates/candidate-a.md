The officer came to the hatch on his own, the car left running behind him with its lights going, red and blue sliding round and round on the wet concrete like something being stirred. He was young too. They were all young now. He had his hat off and held under his arm, which she took for tiredness, not manners.

"Evening," he said, though it was long past that. "You been on all night?"

"Since ten."

"Quiet?"

"Dead," Jo said. "You're my first in an hour."

He looked past her at the shelves she couldn't reach, and past himself at the forecourt, the pumps, the dark round the edges of the light. "We're looking for a man," he said. "On foot, maybe. Bit of a mess, might be. Hurt." He said it like a shopping list, a thing he'd said at the last place and would say at the next. "You've had no one like that."

Round the back, past the bins, out of his lights, a man she had fed and dressed and hidden was standing in a locked toilet with her green fob in his taped hand, and all it would take was the truth, four or five words of it, and she would be back on the right side of everything.

"No one like that," Jo said. "Just you."

The officer nodded. He had not wanted a different answer; it was three in the morning and he had a list. He put a card in the drawer — anything comes to mind — and she slid it back empty-handed and said she would, and he went, and the car swung its stirred light once more round the concrete and pulled out onto the road and took the colour with it, and the night came back down over the forecourt the way water comes back over a dropped stone.

She stood a while. Then she went and did the thing she was not supposed to do on her own at night, which was open the side door and put her head out into the cold. The toilet round the back was dark. The fob was on the ground by the bins where he'd left it, and the man was gone — over the fence, down the embankment, back into whatever had sent him up her verge bleeding, and she would never know now which thing it had been, the running or the standing, the caught-it-on-something or the other thing, the thing she had not let herself think.

She picked up the fob. It was still warm, or she thought it was. She put it in her pocket, and went back in, and locked the door, and behind the glass she started the count of the till she had already counted twice, because there were two hours of dark left and you did what there was to do in them, and somewhere her son was thirty-one and she did not know where, and the road stayed empty a long time.
