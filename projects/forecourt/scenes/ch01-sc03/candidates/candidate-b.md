The officer came to the hatch alone, the car behind him turning its red and blue over the wet concrete.

"You been on all night?"

"Since ten."

"Quiet?"

"Dead. You're my first in an hour."

"We're looking for a man. On foot. Hurt, maybe." He said it flat, a list. "You've had no one."

Round the back, out of his lights, a man she had fed and hidden stood in a locked toilet with her fob in his hand. Four words would undo it.

"No one," Jo said. "Just you."

He put a card in the drawer. She slid it back and said she would. He went. The car took its colour out onto the road, and the dark came back down over the forecourt.

She opened the side door — the thing you did not do, alone, at night — and put her head into the cold. The toilet was dark. The fob lay by the bins where he'd dropped it. He was gone, over the fence and down, back into whatever had put the blood on him, and she would not know now which he had been, the running kind or the standing kind, and she had helped him either way.

She picked up the fob. Warm, or she thought so. She put it in her pocket and went in and locked the door.

Behind the glass she started to count the till she had counted twice already. Two hours of dark left. Somewhere her son was thirty-one and she did not know where. The road stayed empty a long time.
