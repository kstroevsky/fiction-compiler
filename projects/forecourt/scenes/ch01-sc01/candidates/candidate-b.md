Three o'clock and the road dead. Jo sat behind the glass with her thumb a half-inch from the button, the way she sat every night at three — not because of anything, just the hour, the hour when the shop was a lit box on a black road and she was the only thing in it worth the drive.

The man came up on foot from the dark, no car. She was standing before she knew she had stood.

He crossed into the canopy light holding one arm to his chest. Young. A coat that had been good. Blood on the cuff, brown at the edge, red still in the middle — his, then, and not old. That was as much as the glass would give her, and the glass was all there was between them, that and the drawer, and the button under her hand.

"You open?"

"Night hatch." She nodded at the drawer. Her voice did the ordinary thing. Her thumb did not move.

"Have you got a first-aid box. Anything for this." He did not look at the arm. He looked at her, reading her face, and she let him read what a face reads like when it has not decided.

"What happened."

"Caught it on something." Ready. Flat. The answer already made.

She knew that answer. She had given it herself, at counters and in doorways and once to a duty sergeant, about her own boy — caught it on something, walked into something, none of your business what — and she had stood there while people decided whether to believe her, and she had never once forgiven the ones who did not and never once thanked the ones who did. Kieran would be thirty-one now. She had no idea where. He could be at a window like this one, somewhere, saying caught it on something to a woman with her thumb on a button.

The forecourt behind him was empty. The road was empty. One hurt man on foot at three in the morning was a sum with only one safe answer, and she knew the answer, and she got the green box out from under the counter anyway.

She did not give him the box. She put into the drawer what he would need and not a thing more — wipes, gauze, tape, the blue gloves — and slid it, and the tray went through the wall of glass and stopped on his side with its flat clack.

He let a breath go. For a second there was nothing dangerous about him at all, only tiredness and an arm that hurt. Then he took the gloves first, before the gauze, and pulled one on over the bleeding hand, and he did not mean to leave anything of himself behind, and she made herself not know what that meant, and her thumb stayed where it was.
