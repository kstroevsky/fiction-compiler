By three the road had given up. An hour since the last car, a rep who bought forty Bensons and a Twix and wouldn't meet her eye, and after that nothing, only the pumps standing in their own light and the dark laid on everything past them like a lid.

Jo had the radio down low and the till counted twice and the floor she couldn't reach behind the glass gone unswept since ten. This was the good part of the shift, the dead part, three till the first lorries. She sat on the stool with her tea going cold, and the monitor cycled its four grey squares — forecourt, forecourt, the aisles she was locked out of, the back — and she thought about nothing, which was the trick of it.

The man came from the road, not the forecourt. That was the first thing. He came up along the verge out of the dark on foot, no car pulling in, no headlights, just a shape sorting itself into a man as it crossed into the light, and Jo was off the stool before she had decided to be.

He came to the glass. Under the canopy lights he was younger than his walk, thirty maybe, a good coat gone wrong, and he held his left arm across himself, favouring it. There was blood on the cuff. Not a lot. Enough. It had gone brown at the edge and was still red in the middle, so it was his and it was recent, and those were the only two things she knew.

"You open?" His voice came thin through the grille.

"Night hatch," Jo said, and nodded at the drawer, the way she said it forty times a night to drunks and truckers, and her hand had already found the edge of the counter and the button under it without her looking.

He looked at the drawer, then back at her. "I need — have you got a first-aid thing. Plasters. Anything." He was not looking at the blood on his own sleeve. He was looking at her, at her face, checking it for what it was going to do.

"What happened to you." Not soft. A question you asked before you gave a man anything.

"Caught it on something." Flat, ready, a sentence he'd had on the walk up. "It's fine. It's just bleeding."

Caught it on something. She had said worse to less kind faces herself, over the years — about Kieran's knuckles, and Kieran's eye, and the once about Kieran's neck — and every time the person on the other side had chosen to believe her or chosen not to, and either way she had hated them for the half-second it took. She kept her eyes on his hand.

Behind him the forecourt was empty and the road was empty and the cameras were on all of it and none of it would help her if she wanted helping. That was the arithmetic of the night hatch. One man, on his own, hurt, no car, three in the morning: the sum came out one way. Her thumb sat on the button and did not press.

"There's a kit," she said.

She got it from under the counter, the green box with the cross worn off it, and she did not give him the box. She took out what a person needed — the wound wipes, a wad of gauze, the roll of tape, a pair of the blue gloves — and laid them in the drawer and slid it, and the tray went out through the glass to his side with the small hard sound it made, and stopped.

He looked at what was in it. Something went out of him, a breath he had been holding since the verge, and for a second he was only a tired man at a window with his arm hurting, somebody's, and the thing she was not going to let herself feel moved once, under everything, like a fish turning over deep down.

"Thanks," he said. He took the gloves first, which she had not expected, and worked one on over the bad hand, careful, and she understood he did not want to leave anything of himself on the wipes, and she did not let herself think about why a man would not, and her thumb stayed where it was.
