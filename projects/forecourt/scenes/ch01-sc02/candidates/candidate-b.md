He was still on the forecourt, dressing it under the air-line light, when the road gave up its first headlights in an hour.

They came slow from the town end. The man saw them the same second she did. He turned the tap off. He did not run — she'd been ready for the running and it didn't come — he only came back to the glass and put his good hand flat against it, not hard, but flat, a hand the size of the pane, and said, "You didn't see me."

Not a question. Her thumb found the button.

"Say it," he said. "You didn't see anyone."

There are two ways to take a man who says that with his hand on your glass and blood on his other one and a car slowing on the road. She had spent her life taking men the safe way. The safe way was the button under her thumb — one press, and the firm forty miles off would ring the police who were maybe already the ones in the car, and it would be done, and she would be the woman who did it, and whatever he was he would be it in handcuffs by half three.

"There's a toilet round the back," she said. "Out of the cameras. Green fob."

His hand came off the glass.

She got the fob and held it and did not slide it yet. She was thinking about Kieran, and she hated that she was, hated it the way you hate a tooth — an old ache you'd paid to have out years ago and hadn't. Kieran at a glass somewhere with his hand flat on it, saying you didn't see me to some woman who had a button too. She was not being kind. She wanted that understood, if only to herself: there was no kindness in it. It was more like paying a fine in advance for a thing you were going to do anyway.

She slid the fob out. "Round the left. Past the bins."

He took it without a word — no thanks, and she was glad there was no thanks — and went left into the dark, out of the light, and the headlights turned in off the road and came over the forecourt not slowing for any pump, and the blue bar on the roof of it woke once against the canopy, and Jo put her thumb flat on the counter beside the button, not on it, and waited for the knock.
