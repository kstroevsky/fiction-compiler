He dressed it at the tap by the air line, out in the light, his back half to her, working the tape one-handed with his teeth, and she should have told him to move on and did not. She told herself she was making sure he didn't bleed on the pumps. That was a thing she could tell herself.

Then there were headlights on the road.

They came slow from the town side, the way nothing came at this hour, and the man's head came up before hers did. He had the tap running still, and he turned it off, and he stood very straight for a second, gone still all through, and then he did not run. A man who ran was one thing and a man who stood was another, and she did not know yet which he was.

He came back to the glass. "That toilet round the back," he said. "That still work."

Her thumb was on the button. It had been there since the verge, more or less, riding under the pad of her thumb like a coin she was worrying, and all she had to do was let it down. The lights were slowing. If they turned in she would have someone's car on the forecourt in under a minute and a bleeding stranger she'd fed and dressed on her cameras, and the button under her thumb would bring the rest of it — the phone call, the statement, the being the woman who pressed it.

"It's round the left," she said. "Past the bins. The key's a green fob."

She had not known she'd decided that either.

She got the fob off the hook by the safe and she did not put it in the drawer straight away. She held it a second, the man on the other side of the glass with his taped hand and his blood drying brown, his eyes on the road coming to him, and she thought about Kieran, which she had told herself for eleven years she would stop doing — Kieran who was somewhere, who was maybe standing exactly like this at some glass with headlights finding him, and whether the woman behind that glass would push the fob out or push the button. And it was not a kind thought. It had no kindness in it at all. It was closer to a debt, or a bribe she was paying to nobody, so that somebody far off might one day be paid it back.

She put the fob in the drawer and slid it.

"Round the left," she said again. "The light doesn't reach round there."

He took the fob. He looked at her through the glass one more time, that reading look, and whatever he read he did not thank her for this one, and she was glad of the not being thanked. He went left, past the bins, out of the cameras, out of the light, and the headlights turned in off the road and came across the forecourt with the slow certainty of a car not stopping for petrol, and swung, and the blue of it woke up along the canopy struts, once, and Jo took her thumb off the button and put both hands flat on the counter where a person's hands should be.
