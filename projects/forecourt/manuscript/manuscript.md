# Forecourt

## Chapter 1
By three the road had given up. An hour since the last car, a rep who bought forty Bensons and a Twix and wouldn't meet her eye, and after that nothing, only the pumps standing in their own light and the dark laid on everything past them like a lid.

Jo had the radio down low and the till counted twice and the floor she couldn't reach behind the glass gone unswept since ten. This was the good part of the shift, the dead part, three till the first lorries. She sat on the stool with her tea going cold, and the monitor cycled its four grey squares — forecourt, forecourt, the aisles she was locked out of, the back — and she thought about nothing, which was the trick of it.

The man came from the road, not the forecourt. That was the first thing. He was up along the verge out of the dark on foot, no car pulling in, no headlights, just a shape sorting itself into a man as it crossed into the light, and Jo was off the stool before she had decided to be.

He reached the glass. Under the canopy lights he was younger than his walk, thirty maybe, a good coat gone wrong, and he held his left arm across himself, favouring it. There was blood on the cuff. Not a lot. Enough. It had gone brown at the edge and was still red in the middle, so it was his and it was recent, and those were the only two things she knew.

"You open?" His voice came thin through the grille.

"Night hatch," Jo said, and nodded at the drawer, the same nod she gave the drunks and the truckers, and her hand had already found the edge of the counter and the button under it without her looking.

He looked at the drawer, then back at her. "I need — have you got a first-aid thing. Plasters. Anything." He was not looking at the blood on his own sleeve. He was looking at her, at her face, checking it for what it was going to do.

"What happened to you." Not soft. A question you asked before you gave a man anything.

"Caught it on something." Flat, ready, a sentence he'd had on the walk up. "It's fine. It's just bleeding."

Caught it on something. She had said worse to less kind faces herself, over the years — about Kieran's knuckles, and Kieran's eye, and the once about Kieran's neck — and every time the person on the other side had chosen to believe her or chosen not to, and either way she had hated them for the half-second it took. Kieran would be thirty-one now, and she had no idea where. He could be at a glass like this one, somewhere, saying caught it on something to a woman with her thumb on a button. She kept her eyes on the man's hand.

Behind him the forecourt was empty and the road was empty and the cameras were on all of it and none of it would help her if she wanted helping. One man, on his own, hurt, no car, three in the morning: the sum came out one way. Her thumb sat on the button and did not press.

"There's a kit," she said.

She got it from under the counter, the green box with the cross worn off it, and she did not give him the box. She took out what a person needed — the wound wipes, a wad of gauze, the roll of tape, a pair of the blue gloves — and laid them in the drawer and slid it, and the tray went out through the glass to his side with the small hard sound it made, and stopped.

He looked at what was in it. Something went out of him, a breath he had been holding since the verge, and for a second he was only a tired man at a window with his arm hurting, somebody's, and Jo looked down at the drawer and let the second pass.

"Thanks," he said. He took the gloves first, which she had not expected, before the gauze, and worked one on over the bad hand, careful, and she did not let herself think about why a man would put gloves on before he touched his own blood, and her thumb stayed where it was.

· · ·

He dressed it at the tap by the air line, out in the light, his back half to her, working the tape one-handed with his teeth, and she should have told him to move on and did not. She told herself she was making sure he didn't bleed on the pumps. That was a thing she could tell herself.

Then there were headlights on the road.

They came slow from the town side, as nothing came at this hour, and the man's head came up before hers did. He had the tap running still, and he turned it off, and he stood very straight for a second, gone still all through, and then he did not run, and she did not know what to make of that.

He came back to the glass. "That toilet round the back," he said. "That still work."

Her thumb was on the button. It had been there since the verge, more or less, riding under the pad of her thumb like a coin she was worrying, and all she had to do was let it down. The lights were slowing. If they turned in she would have someone's car on the forecourt in under a minute and a bleeding stranger she'd fed and dressed on her cameras, and the button under her thumb would bring the rest of it — the phone call, the statement, the being the woman who pressed it.

"It's round the left," she said. "Past the bins. The key's a green fob."

She had not known she'd decided that either.

She got the fob off the hook by the safe and she did not put it in the drawer straight away. She held it a second, the man on the other side of the glass with his taped hand and his blood drying brown, his eyes on the road coming to him, and she thought about Kieran, which she had told herself for eleven years she would stop doing — Kieran who was somewhere, who was maybe standing exactly like this at some glass with headlights finding him, and whether the woman behind that glass would push the fob out or push the button. And it was not a kind thought. It was closer to a debt, or a bribe she was paying to nobody, so that somebody far off might one day be paid it back.

She put the fob in the drawer and slid it.

"Round the left," she said again. "The light doesn't reach round there."

He took the fob. He looked at her through the glass one more time, that reading look, and whatever he read, he did not thank her for this one, which was how she wanted it. He went left, past the bins, out of the cameras, out of the light, and the headlights turned in off the road and came across the forecourt with the slow certainty of a car not stopping for petrol, and swung, and the blue of it woke up along the canopy struts, once, and Jo took her thumb off the button and put both hands flat on the counter where a person's hands should be.

· · ·

The officer came to the hatch on his own, the car left running behind him with its lights going, red and blue sliding round and round on the wet concrete like something being stirred. He was young, and he had his hat off and held under his arm, which she took for tiredness, not manners.

"Evening," he said, though it was long past that. "You been on all night?"

"Since ten."

"Quiet?"

"Dead," Jo said. "You're my first in an hour."

He looked past her at the shelves she couldn't reach, and past himself at the forecourt, the pumps, the dark round the edges of the light. "We're looking for a man," he said. "On foot, maybe. Bit of a mess, might be. Hurt." He said it like a shopping list, a thing he'd said at the last place and would say at the next. "You've had no one like that."

Round the back, past the bins, out of his lights, a man she had fed and dressed and hidden was standing in a locked toilet with her green fob in his taped hand, and all it would take was the truth, four or five words of it, and she would be out of it — no statement, no cameras, no part in whatever he was.

"No one like that," Jo said. "Just you."

The officer nodded. He had not wanted a different answer; it was three in the morning and he had a list. He put a card in the drawer — anything comes to mind — and she slid it back empty-handed and said she would, and he went, and the car swung its stirred light once more round the concrete and pulled out onto the road and took the colour with it, and the night came back down over the forecourt.

She stood a while. Then she went and did the thing she was not supposed to do on her own at night, which was open the side door and put her head out into the cold. The toilet round the back was dark. The fob was on the ground by the bins where he'd left it, and the man was gone — over the fence, down the embankment, back into whatever had sent him up her verge bleeding, and she would never know now which it had been, the running or the standing, the caught-it-on-something or the other, the one she had not let herself think.

She picked up the fob. It was still warm, or she thought it was. She put it in her pocket and went back in and locked the door. Behind the glass she started the count of the till she had already counted twice. There were two hours of dark left. Somewhere her son was thirty-one and she did not know where. The road stayed empty a long time.
