Under the big light the paint looked better than ever, which was the cruelty of paint. Rennie set the boat on the trestles, Aldous holding the stern though she had not asked him to, and she took the wooden mallet off the bench.

"What's that for."

"Listening." She tapped along the topsides, easy blows, moving forward. The sound came back bright and hard, plank after plank, the sound of wood with something to say. Then she came down onto the garboard by the stem and tapped, and the note went out of it. A flat sound. A knock on a door with no room behind it.

She tapped the sound plank again so he would have the two side by side. Bright. Flat. She did not say anything. She let the sounds stand next to each other.

"Tap anything long enough and it sounds wrong," Aldous said.

Rennie put the mallet down and took up her knife.

"Don't."

"You can do it, if you'd rather. I'll show you where." She held the knife out to him, handle first. He did not take it. She turned back to the boat and set the point to the garboard where the note had died, a hand's width up from the keel, and leaned on it.

The blade went in as if the wood were not there. To the first knuckle, the second, the whole length of the steel swallowed, and when she drew it the wood came with it in wet brown crumbs, and the smell she had caught at the gate came up full — under the turpentine now, the sweet dark of a plank that has been drinking three years under a tarp.

Aldous looked at the hole. It was the first time he had looked at the plank instead of at her.

"That's one place," he said.

"It's every place the water sat." She moved down and pushed her thumb through the paint at the hood end, where the planking dies into the sternpost, and the paint tore like a scab and the wood behind it had gone the colour of tea and gave all the way in. "Your father knew wood better than I ever will. He didn't know about this. It got into her while he was ill — three winters under a tarp with the wet coming through and no one to lift the cover. Not while he had the tools in his hands."

Aldous came off the stern. For a second it seemed he might put his hand on her, and she stood square and let him decide what to do with it. He did not touch her. He turned and hit the shed door with the flat of his hand, once, the sound going out across the yard, and stood with his back to her.

"They're coming at seven," he said. "I stood up at the funeral and told the whole church he'd have her on the water by summer." His voice did a thing and came back level. "You want me to go down and tell them what. That he spent three years painting a dead boat."

Rennie ran the hose over the garboard while his back was to her. She wanted him to hear it. The water sheeted off the good planks and clung, then found the seam over the rot and went dark and beaded through to the inside, one bead and then a run of them, tapping onto the concrete under the trestles.

"Hear that." It was not a question. "That's her in a flat calm, no load, no sea. Put four men and a running tide in her and that seam opens like a mouth. She'll swim ten minutes and go down at the stern, and she'll do it in front of your father's crowd."

The dripping went on into the quiet.

"So that's your answer," he said. "No."

"No's the easy one." Rennie shut the hose off. "I've a harder one, if you want it."

He turned around.

"I can't send her out. Not today, not with a winter's work in her I can't do by seven. But I can put her in." It came out plain and sure, the way a thing does when it is right. "On the top of the tide, on the slack, with lines fore and aft in my hands and every man there knowing what she is. Long enough for the water to take her weight. Long enough that she floats — because she will float, for that. Then we bring her up the slip before she settles. That's not a launch, Aldous. But it is a launching. It is the only true one she has."

He stood with the dead plank between them, the hole in it dark, the water still finding its way onto the concrete.

"Every man there knowing," he said.

"You don't lie to men who knew your father. You put her in honest, or you don't put her in."

For a long moment there was only the drip. Then Aldous wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and looked at the boat, the whole white length of her under the light.

"Get your lines," he said.
