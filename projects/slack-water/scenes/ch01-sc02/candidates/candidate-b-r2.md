Rennie got the boat onto the trestles and turned the big light on her, and under it the white paint was perfect, and that was the trouble.

"You said under the light," Aldous said. "She's under the light. She's sound."

"Give me a minute with her."

"The men are coming at seven."

"Then I've got till seven." She took the mallet from the bench.

He came off the door. "You're not knocking holes in my father's boat."

"I'm knocking on it." She tapped the topside — a hard, bright note — then the garboard by the stem, and the note fell out of it, flat and dead. She tapped the two again, one, the other. "You hear the difference."

"I hear a woman who wants a survey fee."

She let that sit. Then she set the mallet down. "There's no fee in what I'm about to tell you. That's the worst of it."

She put the knife to the dead place at the garboard and leaned, and the blade went in to the handle, the whole length of the steel swallowed. She worked it and drew it, and the wood came out on the steel in wet crumbs, and the smell came up under the turpentine — the sweet dark of a plank left to drink under a wet tarp. She held the blade up between them and said nothing.

"One soft plank." Aldous's voice had come down. "That can be replaced."

"It's the garboard. And the hood ends." She pushed her thumb through the paint where the planking dies into the sternpost, and it went in like a scab, and the wood behind it had gone the colour of tea. "And likely the frames behind them. That is not a plank. That is the winter. New wood from the keel up, six weeks of it, and it is July."

"He painted her in the spring," Aldous said. "He was sick, and he went out to the shed, and he painted her himself. You're telling me he painted a rotten boat."

"I'm telling you it got into her after. Under the tarp, with the wet coming through, and no one to lift the cover off her all winter, while he was ill." She held his eye. "The plank tells me the winter. It doesn't tell me a thing about the man."

He turned from it, to the door, and stood with his back to her.

"They're coming at seven," he said. "I stood up at the funeral and told the whole church he'd have her on the water by summer." His voice caught, and came back level. "You want me to go down and tell them what. That he spent his last years on a dead boat."

Rennie ran the hose over the garboard while his back was to her. The water sheeted off the good planks and clung, then found the seam over the rot and went dark and beaded through to the inside, one bead and then a run of them, tapping onto the concrete under the trestles.

"Hear that." It was not a question. "That's her in a flat calm — no load, no sea. Put four men and a running tide in her and that seam opens. She'll swim ten minutes and go down at the stern, in front of your father's crowd."

The dripping went on into the quiet.

"So that's your answer," he said. "No."

Rennie shut the hose off. The office drawer was in her mind — the chandler's second letter, the diesel — and a launch fee spent no worse than any other money. She could set the trolley under the boat, and let the harbour have its morning, and take the man's money, and hear that seam under the cheering for the rest of her life.

"No's the easy one," she said.

He turned around.

"I can't send her out. Not today, not with a winter's work in her I can't do by seven. But I can put her in — on the top of the tide, on the slack, on lines in my hands, and every man there knowing what she is, because you'll tell them, or I will. She'll take her own weight and float. Long enough for them to see it. Then we bring her up before she settles."

"That's not a launch."

"No. But it's a launching." She coiled the hose back onto the reel. "It's the one she's got."

He stood with the dead plank between them, the hole in it dark, the water still working its way onto the concrete.

"Every man there knowing," he said.

"Honest, or not at all."

For a long moment there was only the drip. Then Aldous wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and looked at the boat, the whole white length of her under the light.

"Get your lines," he said.
