Rennie got the boat onto the trestles and turned the big light on her, and under it the white paint was perfect, and that was the trouble.

"You said under the light," Aldous said. "She's under the light. She's sound."

"Give me a minute with her."

"The men are coming at seven."

"Then I've got till seven." She took the mallet from the bench.

He came off the door. "You're not knocking holes in my father's boat."

"I'm knocking on it." She tapped the topside — a hard, bright note — then the garboard by the stem, and the note fell out of it, flat and dead. She tapped the two again, one, the other. "You hear the difference."

"I hear a woman who wants a survey fee."

She let that sit. Then she set the mallet down. "There's no fee in what I'm about to tell you. That's the trouble with it."

She put the knife to the dead place at the garboard and leaned, and the blade went in to the handle with no more argument than it would give a pat of butter. She worked it and drew it, and the wood came out on the steel in wet crumbs. She held it up between them and said nothing.

"One soft plank." Aldous's voice had come down. "That can be replaced."

"It's the garboard. And the hood ends." She pushed her thumb through the paint at the stern, and it went in as if the paint were a scab. "And likely the frames behind them. That is not a plank. That is the winter. New wood from the keel up, six weeks of it, and it is July."

"He painted her in the spring." He said it as though the paint were the argument, as though she had forgotten it. "He was sick and he went out to the shed and he painted her himself. You're telling me he painted a rotten boat."

"I'm telling you it got into her after. Under the tarp, with the wet coming through, and no one to lift the cover off her all winter." She held his eye. "It happened to her while he was ill. Not while he had the tools in his hands. He would never have let her go in like this."

That reached him. He turned from it, to the door, and stood there.

"So you won't put her in."

Here it was. Rennie thought of the drawer and the chandler, and how a launch fee spent no worse than any other money; of the harbour on the slip at seven, and how easy the cheering would be, and how she would hear that seam under the noise the whole time.

"I'll put her in," she said.

He turned.

"Not to sail. She will not sail again without the work, and I can't do the work by seven, nor by Friday. But I will put her in the water on the top of the tide, on the slack, on lines, in front of every man there — and every man there is going to know she is not sound, because you are going to tell them, or I am. She will float. She will take her own weight and float, long enough for them to see it. Then we bring her up before she settles."

"That's not a launch."

"No. It is a launching. The true one instead of the lying one." She coiled the hose off the reel. "Your father would not have thanked me for the lying one."

Aldous looked at the boat a long time. The water she had run over the garboard was still finding the seam, dripping off the keel onto the concrete, one bead and another, counting off the seconds in the quiet.

"Get your lines," he said.
