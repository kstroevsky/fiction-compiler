The men came while the tide was still making. They came in ones and twos through the gate, old men mostly, in the coats they kept for funerals, and they stood along the top of the slip and did not say much. One of them Rennie knew — Cathal, who had crewed for Tom forty years. He looked at the boat on the trolley, and at Aldous, and at nothing.

Rennie ran the lines herself. One from the stem, one from the quarter, both back to the ring bolt at the head of the slip, both ends in her hands, so she could hold the boat on the water and take her off it when she said.

She spoke to Aldous without turning from the boat. "When she's in, you'll have a minute. Maybe two. Don't make me tell you when."

"I'll know when," he said.

Slack water came near seven, the estuary going flat and full, the pause before the tide turns. Rennie nodded, and two of the younger men eased the trolley down the greased ways, and the Kittiwake went down stern-first into her own reflection, lifted off the cradle, and floated.

She floated. There was that. She sat on the flat water with the low sun on her white topsides and the gold name, and along the slip the men stopped talking, and for a moment the boat was only a boat on the water, and fifty years of Tom Vane were floating in front of the men who had known him.

Rennie read the boat the way she had read the plank. Then the water found the seam — not outside, inside, low in the bilge, a darkening along the garboard where there should have been dry wood, a wet line that was not there and then was, and then was climbing.

No one along the slip could see it. Only Rennie, and Aldous, who had come down to the water's edge and was looking where she looked.

"There," she said.

He went down on one knee on the wet stone, the boat riding beside him, and put his hand flat on the plank below the name, where Rennie had put hers in the dark that morning. He held it there. On the other side of the wood the water climbed to meet his hand.

"All right," he said. Not to Rennie. To the boat, maybe, or the man who built her.

Rennie took the strain on the lines. "Now." The young men had the trolley waiting, and they walked the Kittiwake up out of the water onto her cradle, and she came up streaming, the estuary running off the keel and out through the garboard seam in a steady bright thread.

The men helped run her up the yard. No one cheered; it was not that kind of morning. Cathal put a hand on Aldous's shoulder as he passed and said something Rennie did not catch, and Aldous nodded, and it was done.

Rennie went into the office and wrote the ticket: the survey, the launch-and-haul, the trolley time. Not the restoration. Not the six weeks of new plank the boat would never get. It came to less than a launch fee. She stood in the doorway with it a moment, the diesel bill still in the drawer under her hand.

She brought him the ticket. He read it and reached for his back pocket.

"She stays till you decide about her," Rennie said. "No charge for a boat standing in the yard."

Aldous folded the ticket into his coat and looked at the Kittiwake up on her cradle, the wet line at her garboard going pale as she gave the water back.

"She floated," he said.

"She floated."

He got in the truck and drove out. Rennie went to turn the cover back over the boat, and stopped, the tarp in her hands. The sun was coming over the sheds and onto her, onto the white hull and the drying seam. She left the cover off. The boat could dry.
