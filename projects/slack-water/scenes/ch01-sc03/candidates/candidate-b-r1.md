The men came up the slip while the tide was still making. Old men, in their funeral coats. They stood along the top and waited and did not say much.

Rennie ran the lines herself. One from the stem, one from the quarter, both back to the ring bolt at the head of the slip, both ends in her hands, so she could hold the boat on the water and take her off it when she said.

"You'll have a minute when she's in," she told Aldous. "Not much more."

"A minute's enough."

Slack water came near seven. The estuary went flat and full and still. She gave the word, and the young men eased the trolley down the ways, and the Kittiwake went into the water stern-first, came off her cradle, and floated.

She floated. The white of her sat on the flat grey, and the gold name held the light, and along the top of the slip the men went quiet. Tom Vane's boat, on the water.

Then the seam. Inside, low, a dark line along the garboard where the water was coming in, a wet thread climbing the dry wood. Only Rennie had the eye for it — and Aldous, beside her now at the water's edge, who had learned that morning where to look.

He went down on one knee on the wet stone and put his hand on the plank, below the name, where the paint was cold. He left it there while the water rose to meet it through the wood.

"All right," he said. To the boat. To no one.

"Now." Rennie took the strain.

They walked her back up out of the water before she could settle, and she came up streaming, the estuary pouring off the keel and out of the garboard seam in a thin bright line.

No one cheered. Cathal, who had crewed for Tom forty years, put a hand on Aldous's shoulder going past, and that was all.

In the office Rennie wrote the ticket. Survey. Launch and haul. The trolley time. Not the boat she would not build him by summer. It came to less than the launch alone would have. The diesel bill was still in the drawer.

"She stays in the yard till you decide," she said, bringing it out. "No charge for standing."

Aldous put the ticket in his coat.

"She floated," he said.

"She did."

He drove out. Rennie went to cover the boat and stopped, the tarp in her hands. The sun was coming over the sheds, onto the white hull and the pale wet line at the garboard.

She left the cover off. The boat could dry.
