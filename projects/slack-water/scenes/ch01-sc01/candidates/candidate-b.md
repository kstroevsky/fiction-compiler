Rennie was rolling the big door back on its track when the trailer came in at the gate. Not yet six. The tide would make at seven.

Aldous Vane climbed down and set his hand on the boat's cradle, steadying it, though it had already stopped rocking. "I want her launched on this tide," he said. No good morning to it.

"That's her, then." Rennie came down the apron wiping her hands. Sixteen foot, clinker-built, white as a wedding. Gold on the transom: Kittiwake. Tom Vane's three winters in the far shed, and Rennie had never been let in to see the work.

"Half seven the men will be here. Dad's crowd." Aldous took a breath to say more, and didn't.

"Who rigged her?"

"She's not for rigging. She's for launching." He closed the subject the way his father used to close a subject. "Ten minutes with the trolley. You've done it a hundred times."

She had. Never once with a boat nobody had floated. Rennie walked her length. The paint was new and the paint was good — you could have shaved in it. That was the first thing wrong with it.

"I'll survey her before she goes down the slip."

"There's nothing to survey. You knew my father."

"That's why."

She set her hand on the garboard. Hard, cold, sound all the way to the stem — and then, three planks aft, the give. A softness that took the ball of her thumb and held it. She stopped there.

Aldous kept his eyes on her hand, not the plank. "It's fresh paint. Soft because it's paint."

"Paint doesn't give, Aldous."

"He painted her last spring." He said it again, most of it, quieter. "He painted her himself."

Rennie thought of the office drawer and what was in it — the chandler's second letter, the bill for the diesel — and how far one launch fee would go against them. It would be no trouble at all to run the trolley under a boat a man was asking her to launch, and let the harbour cheer, and take his money for it.

She pressed the plank again. Again it gave.

"Cut the engine," she said. "Bring her under the light. I'm not saying no. I'm saying I have to look first."

For a moment he stood. Then he leaned in and killed it, and the yard went quiet enough to hear the tide coming up the slip, stone over stone, and neither of them said aloud what the quiet was for.
