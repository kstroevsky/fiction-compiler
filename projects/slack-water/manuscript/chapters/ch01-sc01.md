Rennie was rolling the big door back on its track when the trailer came in at the gate, too fast, and stopped with the boat rocking on its cradle. Not yet six. The tide would make at seven.

Aldous Vane climbed down and left the engine running. He had his father in the shoulders and nowhere else. "I want her launched on this tide," he said. No good morning to it.

"That's her, then." Rennie came down the apron wiping her hands. Sixteen foot, clinker-built, painted a flat hard white, the bootline struck clean, the name in gold on the transom: Kittiwake. Tom Vane's three winters in the far shed, and Rennie had never been let in to see the work.

"Half seven the men will be here. Dad's crowd." Aldous took a breath to say more, and didn't.

"Who rigged her?"

"She's not for rigging. She's for launching." He closed the subject the way his father used to close a subject. "Ten minutes with the trolley. You've done it a hundred times."

She had. Never once with a boat nobody had floated. Rennie walked her length. The paint was new and the paint was good — you could have shaved in it.

"I'll survey her before she goes down the slip."

"There's nothing to survey. You knew my father."

"That's why."

She set her hand on the garboard, low against the keel, and ran it aft. Hard, cold, sound — and then, back where the plank dies into the sternpost, the give. A softness that took the ball of her thumb and held it. She stopped there.

Aldous kept his eyes on her hand, not the plank. "It's fresh paint. Soft because it's paint."

"Paint doesn't give, Aldous."

"He painted her last spring." He said it again, most of it. "He painted her himself."

Rennie thought of the office drawer — the chandler's second letter, the bill for the diesel — and of how simple it would be to run the trolley under the boat, and let the harbour cheer, and take the man's money, and say nothing at all about the plank.

She pressed it again. Again it gave.

"Cut the engine," she said. "Bring her under the light. I'm not saying no."

For a moment he stood. Then he leaned in and killed it, and in the new quiet the tide came on up the slip, stone over stone, and Rennie stood with her thumb still on the soft of the plank.
