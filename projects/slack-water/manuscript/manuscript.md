# Slack Water

## Chapter 1
Rennie was rolling the big door back on its track when the trailer came in at the gate, too fast, and stopped with the boat rocking on its cradle. Not yet six. The tide would make at seven.

Aldous Vane climbed down and left the engine running. He had his father in the shoulders and nowhere else. "I want her launched on this tide," he said. No good morning to it.

"That's her, then." Rennie came down the apron wiping her hands. Sixteen foot, clinker-built, painted a flat hard white, the bootline struck clean, the name in gold on the transom: Kittiwake. Tom Vane's three winters in the far shed, and Rennie had never been let in to see the work.

"Half seven the men will be here. Dad's crowd." Aldous took a breath to say more, and didn't.

"Who rigged her?"

"She's not for rigging. She's for launching." He closed the subject the way his father used to close a subject. "Ten minutes with the trolley. You've done it a hundred times."

She had. Never once with a boat nobody had floated. Rennie walked her length. The paint was new and the paint was good — you could have shaved in it.

"I'll survey her before she goes down the slip."

"There's nothing to survey. You knew my father."

"That's why."

She set her hand on the garboard, low against the keel, and ran it aft. Hard, cold, sound — and then, back where the plank dies into the sternpost, the give. A softness that took the ball of her thumb and held it. She stopped there.

Aldous kept his eyes on her hand, not the plank. "It's fresh paint. Soft because it's paint."

"Paint doesn't give, Aldous."

"He painted her last spring." He said it again, most of it. "He painted her himself."

Rennie thought of the office drawer — the chandler's second letter, the bill for the diesel — and of how simple it would be to run the trolley under the boat, and let the harbour cheer, and take the man's money, and say nothing at all about the plank.

She pressed it again. Again it gave.

"Cut the engine," she said. "Bring her under the light. I'm not saying no."

For a moment he stood. Then he leaned in and killed it, and in the new quiet the tide came on up the slip, stone over stone, and Rennie stood with her thumb still on the soft of the plank.

· · ·

Rennie got the boat onto the trestles and turned the big light on her, and under it the white paint was perfect, and that was the trouble.

"You said under the light," Aldous said. "She's under the light. She's sound."

"Give me a minute with her."

"The men are coming at seven."

"Then I've got till seven." She took the mallet from the bench.

He came off the door. "You're not knocking holes in my father's boat."

"I'm knocking on it." She tapped the topside — a hard, bright note — then the garboard by the stem, and the note fell out of it, flat and dead. She tapped the two again, one, the other. "You hear the difference."

"I hear a woman who wants a survey fee."

She let that sit. Then she set the mallet down. "There's no fee in what I'm about to tell you. That's the worst of it."

She put the knife to the dead place at the garboard and leaned, and the blade went in to the handle, the whole length of the steel swallowed. She worked it and drew it, and the wood came out on the steel in wet crumbs, and the smell came up under the turpentine — the sweet dark of a plank left to drink under a wet tarp. She held the blade up between them and said nothing.

"One soft plank." Aldous's voice had come down. "That can be replaced."

"It's the garboard. And the hood ends." She pushed her thumb through the paint where the planking dies into the sternpost, and it went in like a scab, and the wood behind it had gone the colour of tea. "And likely the frames behind them. That is not a plank. That is the winter. New wood from the keel up, six weeks of it, and it is July."

"He painted her in the spring," Aldous said. "He was sick, and he went out to the shed, and he painted her himself. You're telling me he painted a rotten boat."

"I'm telling you it got into her after. Under the tarp, with the wet coming through, and no one to lift the cover off her all winter, while he was ill." She held his eye. "The plank tells me the winter. It doesn't tell me a thing about the man."

He turned from it, to the door, and stood with his back to her.

"They're coming at seven," he said. "I stood up at the funeral and told the whole church he'd have her on the water by summer." His voice caught, and came back level. "You want me to go down and tell them what. That he spent his last years on a dead boat."

Rennie ran the hose over the garboard while his back was to her. The water sheeted off the good planks and clung, then found the seam over the rot and went dark and beaded through to the inside, one bead and then a run of them, tapping onto the concrete under the trestles.

"Hear that." It was not a question. "That's her in a flat calm — no load, no sea. Put four men and a running tide in her and that seam opens. She'll swim ten minutes and go down at the stern, in front of your father's crowd."

The dripping went on into the quiet.

"So that's your answer," he said. "No."

Rennie shut the hose off. The office drawer was in her mind — the chandler's second letter, the diesel — and a launch fee spent no worse than any other money. She could set the trolley under the boat, and let the harbour have its morning, and take the man's money, and hear that seam under the cheering for the rest of her life.

"No's the easy one," she said.

He turned around.

"I can't send her out. Not today, not with a winter's work in her I can't do by seven. But I can put her in — on the top of the tide, on the slack, on lines in my hands, and every man there knowing what she is, because you'll tell them, or I will. She'll take her own weight and float. Long enough for them to see it. Then we bring her up before she settles."

"That's not a launch."

"No. But it's a launching." She coiled the hose back onto the reel. "It's the one she's got."

He stood with the dead plank between them, the hole in it dark, the water still working its way onto the concrete.

"Every man there knowing," he said.

"Honest, or not at all."

For a long moment there was only the drip. Then Aldous wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and looked at the boat, the whole white length of her under the light.

"Get your lines," he said.

· · ·

The men came up the slip while the tide was still making. Old men, in their funeral coats. They stood along the top and waited and did not say much.

Rennie ran the lines herself. One from the stem, one from the quarter, both back to the ring bolt at the head of the slip, both ends in her hands, so she could hold the boat on the water and take her off it when she said.

"You'll have a minute when she's in," she told Aldous. "Not much more."

"A minute's enough."

Slack water came near seven. The estuary went flat and full and still. She gave the word, and the young men eased the trolley down the ways, and the Kittiwake went into the water stern-first, came off her cradle, and floated.

She floated. The white of her sat on the flat grey, and the gold name held the light, and along the top of the slip the men went quiet. Tom Vane's boat, on the water.

Then the seam. Inside, low, a dark line along the garboard where the water was coming in, a wet thread climbing the dry wood. Only Rennie had the eye for it — and Aldous, beside her now at the water's edge, who had learned that morning where to look.

He went down on one knee on the wet stone and put his hand on the plank, below the name, where the paint was cold. He left it there while the water rose to meet it through the wood.

"All right," he said. To the boat. To no one.

"Now." Rennie took the strain.

They walked her back up out of the water before she could settle, and she came up streaming, the estuary pouring off the keel and out of the garboard seam in a thin bright line.

No one cheered. Cathal, who had crewed for Tom forty years, put a hand on Aldous's shoulder going past, and that was all.

In the office Rennie wrote the ticket. Survey. Launch and haul. The trolley time. Not the boat she would not build him by summer. It came to less than the launch alone would have. The diesel bill was still in the drawer.

"She stays in the yard till you decide," she said, bringing it out. "No charge for standing."

Aldous put the ticket in his coat.

"She floated," he said.

"She did."

He drove out. Rennie went to cover the boat and stopped, the tarp in her hands. The sun was coming over the sheds, onto the white hull and the pale wet line at the garboard.

She left the cover off. The boat could dry.
