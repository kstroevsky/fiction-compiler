The tide would be full at seven. Rennie had the big doors open on their track and the first grey coming up off the water when the trailer turned in at the gate, too fast, and stopped with the boat rocking on its cradle.

Aldous Vane got out and left the engine running. He had his father in the shoulders and nowhere else. "She's ready," he said. "I want her in on this tide."

The boat was a sixteen-foot clinker launch, and someone had painted her the way you paint a thing you love and cannot finish — two coats of white so even it looked poured, the bootline struck clean, the name lettered in gold on the transom. Kittiwake. Tom had been three winters on her in the far shed and had never once let Rennie past the door.

"Morning to you, too," Rennie said. She wiped her hands down her apron out of habit; they were already clean. "Who's coming?"

"Dad's crowd. Half the harbour. Seven o'clock." He said it like a dare. "You've got the trolley. It's ten minutes' work."

It was ten minutes' work to put a sound boat down the slip. Rennie walked the length of the trailer without answering him, and the smell reached her before her hand did — under the clean turpentine of new paint, something else, faint and sweetish, the breath a shed takes on when the wet has been in it a winter and no one has opened the door.

"I'll want to look at her first," she said.

"She doesn't need looking at. He built boats fifty years."

"Then it won't take me long."

She laid her palm flat on the garboard, the low plank that runs against the keel and does the hardest of the work, the one that lives in the water. The paint was hard and good under her hand. She ran her thumb along the seam, pressing as she went, the way you press along the line of a rib, and three strakes back from the stem the wood gave. Not much. A softness through the skin of the paint, there and gone under the ball of her thumb.

She kept her hand where it was.

"Well?" Aldous had come round the trailer. He would not look at the plank; he kept his eyes on her hand on it. "It's paint. He painted her last spring."

"I know he did." Rennie pressed again, an inch down the seam, and the wood gave in the same soft way, and she took her hand off the boat and stepped back before she went looking for a third.

Behind her the estuary was filling, the mud going under. Ten past six by the shed clock. She thought of the diesel bill folded in the office drawer, and the two quarters she was behind the chandler, and of Tom Vane, who had taught her to strike a bootline when she was nineteen and had kept his shed door shut to her ever since.

"Bring her in out of the wet," Rennie said. "I'll get the light on her."

"There's no time to get the light on her. The tide —"

"There's time for this." She had the safety chain off already. "You brought her to me. Let me do the one thing you brought me for."

He stood with the engine running behind him and the gold name on the transom between them. After a while he reached in and shut it off. In the new quiet the water went on coming up the slip, and under her thumb the place where the plank had given stayed with her, the way the skin goes on remembering a splinter after it is drawn.
