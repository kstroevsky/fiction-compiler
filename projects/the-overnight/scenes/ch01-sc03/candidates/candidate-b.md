The loaves came out at half five. Twelve. Lower in the tin than Esther's, darker at the crust, and when she cut the split one the crumb was tighter, sweeter, good. She ate the heel and did not decide what it meant.

At six the door bolt turned in the empty shop. Halloran came in with the cold on him, collar up.

A regular. The Saturday seed loaf, the wife he used to send for it, then these last weeks himself. He looked at the bags on the counter without quite looking at them.

"Twelve," she said. "Just cooled."

"Right." He worked a note out of his wallet. "She always said this was the only place that got the crust right."

Nadia made his change and did not tell him whose crust it was. Nothing would land tonight. He was doing the next thing on a list — buy the bread — and the bread was there, and that was all the shop was to him this morning.

"Card's in the top bag," she said. "Warm them ten minutes. They come back like fresh."

He nodded, took the bags up in both hands, and went out. The door swung to. The street was going gray.

She took the split loaf off the rack and set it on Esther's board. Then she found a blank card and wrote the bread out in her own hand — the scald, the extra yeast, the warm rise, the tin, the heat, the time. Milk loaf, quick, at the top. Her own name under it. She put it in the box behind Esther's and started the mixer for the morning.
