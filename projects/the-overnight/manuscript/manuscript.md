# The Overnight

## Chapter 1
At two the ovens were cold and the shop belonged to no one but Nadia. She had turned the sign an hour before and locked the front. Now the only light was the low bulb over the bench and the green figures on the proofer's dial across the room. Flour hung in the air from the day's bakes and settled on her forearms while she worked, a fine gray she no longer brushed away.

Twelve tins stood in a row on the bench, greased and lined. Twelve was the Halloran order, milk loaves for the wake, due at the counter by six. Esther had taken the order herself, weeks back, in her small upright hand in the book. Then Esther had climbed a stepladder she had been told to leave alone, and now she lay three streets over in a bed with a rail on it, and the overnight belonged to Nadia.

Everything had gone by the book. The flour she scaled twice to be sure. The milk she scalded and let cool to blood heat against her wrist. At eight the dough went into the walk-in to proof slow, the way the milk loaf had to, hours in the cold to build its soft crumb. After that the work was to leave it be. That was the hard part of Esther's bread, the leaving be.

Now it was time to knock the dough back and shape.

The walk-in breathed its cold over her when she opened the door. She reached for the tub on the middle shelf, and the give of it was wrong before she had it clear. The cloth on top had sunk. Under it the dough had climbed the sides and fallen, a slack tide gone to the rim and over, and what rose off it was not the smell of bread. It was sharp, sour beneath, the smell of yeast that had eaten everything and turned on itself.

Off came the cloth. The surface was pocked gray with burst bubbles. Two fingers pressed in, and the dough took the dents and held them, wet and slack and dead. Over-proofed. Gone.

The dial across the room only confirmed it. Its needle sat well above the mark. Overnight the old thermostat had drifted, the way it always drifted, and Esther was not there to bring it down by hand three times a shift with a rag laid over the vent. Nadia had checked it at ten. She had not gone back.

She stood with the tub against her hip and did the arithmetic she did not want. Six was four hours off. Esther's dough was an eight-hour dough. No arrangement of the next four hours turned this ruin back into twelve loaves for a man to carry into his wife's funeral.

The tub was heavy and cold. She set it in the sink, ran the tap, and the water took a long ribbon of the sour smell down the drain. Then the tap went quiet under her hand. The twelve tins waited in their row, greased and lined, for bread that would not come out of that walk-in.

· · ·

The phone was in her hand before she chose to pick it up. Esther's name sat at the top of the list, and under her thumb the screen threw a hard blue light across the flour on the bench. Three streets over the ward would be dark. A nurse would go down the row and wake her, and Esther would surface out of whatever the fall and the drugs had left her, and she would say, in the voice she kept for a scaled measure read back wrong, tell me exactly what you did.

Nadia stood with the light in her hand and the ruined tub in the sink and the twelve tins waiting.

She set the phone face down on the bench.

There was a milk bread she could make in the hours she had. Not Esther's. Her own, or near enough — a straight dough, more yeast, a warm fast rise by the oven mouth instead of the long cold one. It would come out plainer. Tighter crumb, less of that pull Esther's loaf gave when you tore it. But it would be bread, and it would be twelve, and it would stand at the counter by six.

She scalded fresh milk, and this time she stood over the pan. The first scald she had ever done she rushed, weeks ago, learning; she took it past the skin and Esther made her pour the pan down the drain and start again. Milk's dear, Esther said. Waste is a decision. Tonight she caught it at the shiver before the boil and pulled it, and let it fall to blood heat while she scaled the flour.

Her hands ran the measures without her having to send them. That was the strange part, and she let it be strange. Flour, salt, the sugar that would feed the fast yeast, the yeast itself bloomed in a spoon of the warm milk until it foamed at the rim. She made the well and poured and drew it together with the flat of her hand, and the dough came up under her the way dough came, a live weight that leaned back against the push.

She turned it onto the bench and put the heels of her palms into it. Push, fold, quarter turn. Push, fold, quarter turn. The wood took the rhythm up through her arms and shoulders. Somewhere in the middle of it the arithmetic stopped running. There was the dough going from ragged to smooth, from slack to a skin that sprang back at a fingertip, coming alive the way the other batch had stopped being alive.

She oiled a bowl, set the dough by the oven, and lit the oven low to throw its heat into the corner. Ten to three by the clock over the door. A fast dough would rise warm in an hour, and she had the hour. Knock it back, shape it, a short second prove in the tins, and it would go into the heat with time to spare before six.

The phone lay face down where she had left it. She did not turn it over.

· · ·

The loaves came out at half five, twelve of them, and she turned them onto the rack to cool. They were not Esther's loaves. They stood lower in the tin, the crust a shade darker where the fast sugar caught, and the crumb — she cut the end off the one that had split and tried it — tighter, closer, sweeter than it should have been and good. She ate the heel standing up and did not decide anything about it.

At six the bolt on the front door turned over loud in the empty shop. She had unlocked it a minute before and gone back to bag the order, and now Halloran came in out of the dark with his collar up and the cold still on him.

He was a big man gone quiet. A regular — the Saturday seed loaf, the wife he used to send in for it, and then, these last weeks, himself. He stood at the counter and looked at the bags without quite looking at them.

"Twelve," she said. "For eleven o'clock. They're just cooled."

"Right." He got his wallet out and worked a note loose. "She always said this was the only place that got the crust right."

Nadia took the note and counted his change and did not tell him whose crust it was tonight. There was nothing to say that would land. He was somewhere she could not follow, doing the next thing on a list someone had set in front of him — buy the bread — and the bread was there, twelve of them bagged, and that was the whole of what he needed from the shop this morning.

"There's a card in the top bag," she said. "Warm them ten minutes before. They come back like fresh."

He nodded at that. He took the bags up in both hands, the way you carry a thing you have been trusted with, and he went out, and the door swung to behind him, and the street past the glass was going gray.

She stood there. Then she went to the rack and took the last loaf, the split one she had cut, and set it on the board where Esther kept hers.

The recipe box lived by the till. She found a blank card and Esther's pen and wrote it out in her own hand — the milk scalded and cooled, the extra yeast, the warm rise at the oven, the tin, the heat, the time. At the top she wrote milk loaf, quick. Under it, small, she wrote her own name. Then she set the card in the box behind Esther's, where whoever kept the next overnight alone would find it, and she started the mixer for the morning bake.
